Open your credit report and look at the status against each loan and credit card. One word sits there — *closed*, *settled*, *written off* — and it does more to decide your next loan application than almost anything else on the page.

People use the three interchangeably. Lenders do not. To a lending officer they describe three very different endings, and mixing them up is how borrowers end up confused about why they keep getting refused.

## Closed

**Closed means you repaid everything you owed and the account was shut normally.**

You took the loan, you paid every instalment, the last payment cleared the balance, and the lender closed the account. Or you paid off your credit card and asked for it to be shut. Nothing was written down, nothing was waived, nobody lost money.

This is the status you want. A file full of closed accounts with clean payment histories is the strongest thing a credit report can say about you: this person borrows and pays it back.

Closed accounts do not vanish from your report, and you should not want them to. A well-paid loan that closed three years ago is still evidence in your favour.

## Settled

**Settled means the lender accepted less than the full amount to close the account.**

This happens when repayment has gone wrong. Perhaps you lost your job, or the EMIs became unmanageable, and after some months of non-payment the lender offered a one-time settlement: pay this reduced amount and we will close the account. You paid it. The account shut.

From your side, it can feel like the matter was dealt with properly. You negotiated, you paid what was agreed, you did not run away. That is fair, and it is far better than never paying at all.

From the lender's side, the record says something else: this borrower did not repay in full, and we took a loss to close the account. A settled status is a warning sign — not the worst one, but a clear one, and it affects what you are offered and at what rate.

That mismatch between how it feels and how it reads catches a lot of people out.

## Written off

**Written off means the lender gave up on recovering the money.**

When an account goes unpaid for a long stretch — usually somewhere around six months — the bank stops treating that money as something it expects to get back. It moves the outstanding amount off its active books for accounting purposes and reports the account to the bureaus as written off.

Two things about this are worth saying very plainly, because they are where most people go wrong.

**It does not cancel your debt.** Writing off is a bookkeeping step inside the bank, not forgiveness. The lender can still ask you for the money, and the account stays in your credit history where every future lender can see it.

**It is one of the most damaging entries a report can carry.** A missed EMI says you were late once. Written off says a bank concluded you were never going to pay at all. Many lenders' systems flag it automatically and decline the application without a human ever reading the rest of the file.

## The order of damage

Put simply:

| Status | What it says | How lenders read it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Closed | Repaid in full, account shut normally | Good |
| Settled | Lender accepted less than the full amount | A partial loss — a clear warning |
| Written off | Lender abandoned recovery | The most damaging of the three |

There are other statuses you may see — *suit filed* where the lender took legal action, or accounts marked as passed to a recovery agency. Those sit alongside written off in terms of how hard they stop an application.

## Where the errors creep in

Here is the part that matters most, and the reason we spend so much of our time on these three words.

**Reports quite often show the harshest of the three when a milder one is what actually happened.**

The common patterns:

**You settled, but the report says written off.** The lender agreed a settlement, you paid it, and somebody used the wrong status code. You are judged as though you walked away when in fact you negotiated and paid.

**You paid in full, but the report says settled.** More common than it should be. Sometimes it follows a period of trouble where a settlement was discussed and then you cleared the whole amount instead.

**You cleared everything, but the status was never updated.** The dues were paid, the lender took the money, and nobody sent the closure update to the bureau. The account still reads written off with a balance outstanding.

**The account is closed but still shows a balance.** That inflates how much you appear to owe and quietly reduces what lenders think you can afford.

Every one of these is a factual error. And a factual error is exactly what the dispute process exists for.

## What can be corrected, and what cannot

Let us be honest about the line, because plenty of people in this business are not.

**A wrong status can be disputed.** If you have proof — a settlement letter, payment receipts, a bank statement showing the money leaving your account, a No Objection Certificate — then a report saying something different from what your documents show is an error. You can raise it with the bureau and the lender, and it can be corrected. Every borrower in India has that right, the process is official, and it costs nothing to use directly.

**A correct status cannot be deleted.** If you genuinely settled for less than you owed, *settled* is what happened, and no service can have that changed to *closed* simply because it is unflattering. If you genuinely stopped paying and the money is still outstanding, *written off* is an accurate record. Anyone promising to remove a true entry for a fee is selling something they cannot deliver — and the honest version of that conversation is worth more to you than the promise.

One nuance people ask about often: if you now pay the full amount that was waived in a settlement, the status does not usually flip to *closed*. What should update is the balance and the fact that the outstanding is cleared. The history of what happened generally stays. Paying still helps, and lenders read a cleared account very differently from an unpaid one — but expecting the word to change will only lead to disappointment.

## What to do next

Pull your credit report and read the status against every account. Not the score — the words.

For each one, ask: does this match what actually happened? If a loan you finished shows as settled, or an account you cleared still reads written off, that gap between your documents and the report is the whole case. Find the settlement letter, the NOC, the receipts, the statement. If you have lost the NOC, you can usually still ask the lender for it years later.
