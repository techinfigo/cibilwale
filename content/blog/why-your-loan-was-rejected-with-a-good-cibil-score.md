It is a particularly frustrating kind of rejection. You checked your score before applying, it was 780, you felt confident — and the bank still said no. Nobody explained why.

If that has happened to you, the thing worth understanding is this: **the score is not the application.** It is one input among several, and a good one does not override everything else a lender looks at.

## Your income does not support the EMI

Lenders work out how much of your monthly income already goes on loan repayments. Add the EMI you are asking for. If the total crosses their comfort level, the application fails — no matter how perfectly you have paid everything so far.

A good score says you repay reliably. It says nothing about whether you can afford another instalment on top of what you already carry. Someone with a spotless record and four running loans can be refused where someone with a thinner file and no existing EMIs is approved.

## Your credit utilisation is high

Utilisation is how much of your available credit limit you are actually using. If your cards total ₹2 lakh in limits and you are carrying ₹1.6 lakh, that is 80% utilisation.

You can pay every bill on time and still sit high on utilisation month after month. A lender reading the report sees someone living close to their limit, and that reads as financial pressure even when your payment record is flawless.

Bringing balances down before applying often does more for an application than anything else you can change quickly.

## You applied to several lenders at once

Every time you apply for credit, the lender pulls your report, and that is recorded as a hard enquiry. A cluster of them in a short period looks like someone desperately hunting for credit, and it is read that way.

This traps people after a first rejection. They are refused, so they try three more banks, each adds an enquiry, and the file looks worse with every attempt. If you have been refused, find out why before applying anywhere else.

## Your details did not match

Lenders verify what you write on the application against what the bureau holds — name, PAN, date of birth, address.

If your report has a different spelling of your surname, an old address, or a PAN with two digits transposed, the file can stall at verification. You will rarely be told this was the reason.

These mismatches are worth fixing whether or not you are applying for anything, because they are also how a stranger's loan ends up attached to your file.

## The report shows something the score has already absorbed

This is the one people miss most often.

A score is a summary. It can recover over time while the specific entry that damaged it is still sitting in the report, visible to anyone reading it.

So you can have a score of 760 and still have a **written off** account from four years ago, or a **settled** status, or a **suit filed** entry. Many lenders' systems scan for exactly those flags and decline on sight, whatever the number says.

If you have been refused with a good score, this is the first thing to check. Do not look at the number. Look at the status against every account.

## Something on the report is not yours

Bureaus match records using a combination of PAN, name, date of birth and address. When a lender uploads a file with any of those wrong, the record can attach to whoever else matches — and if your name is common in your city, the odds go up considerably.

The result is a loan you never took sitting in your file, counted in your borrowing, sometimes carrying a default that belongs to a stranger. An account you do not recognise is also sometimes an early sign of identity fraud, so it is worth ruling out quickly.

## A closed loan is still showing as running

You finished a loan two years ago and the report still shows it as active, possibly with a balance. That phantom EMI is counted when the lender works out what you can afford, so you are assessed as though you still carry a repayment you finished long ago.

## Your credit history is thin

A high score built on one card and eighteen months of history is not the same as one built on a decade of varied borrowing. Some lenders want a longer track record, particularly for large or unsecured loans. Nothing is wrong here and nothing needs disputing — it simply takes time.

## The lender's own rules

Finally, and this frustrates people most: a bank may decline you for reasons that have nothing to do with your credit record at all.

Minimum income for that product. Your employer not on their approved list. Your job type. Your city or pin code. Their appetite for a kind of lending that month. Every lender sets its own rules, and they change.

This is why a rejection from one bank does not mean much on its own. The same file can be declined by one lender and approved by another the same week.

## What to do about it

**Get your full credit report, not just the score.** The number cannot tell you which of the reasons above applies to you. The report can. You are entitled to a free full report from each bureau once a year.

**Check more than one bureau.** India has four — CIBIL TransUnion, Experian, Equifax and CRIF High Mark — and a lender may check any of them. An error on the one you have never looked at is still costing you applications.

**Read the personal information section first.** Name, PAN, date of birth, addresses. This is where verification failures and mixed-up accounts begin.

**Read the status on every account.** Written off, settled, suit filed, or an account still showing active when you closed it years ago.

**Stop applying until you know.** Every further attempt adds an enquiry and makes the next one slightly harder.
