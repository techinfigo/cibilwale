Most people in India first hear about their credit score when a bank turns them down. The officer says the score is low, explains little more, and that is the end of it.

You do not have to wait for a bank to tell you. You can look at your own credit record whenever you like, and the first look each year costs nothing.

## You have four credit reports, not one

Before anything else, this surprises almost everyone: India has four licensed credit bureaus, and each keeps its own separate file on you.

- **CIBIL TransUnion** — the one most people mean when they say "my CIBIL score"
- **Experian**
- **Equifax**
- **CRIF High Mark**

Each bureau builds its file from whatever the lenders chose to send it, so the four are rarely identical. An account can appear on one and not another.

A lender may check any one of them, and you do not get to choose which. So if you are serious about knowing where you stand, look at more than one.

## The free annual report

Each bureau is required to make one full credit report available to you free of charge every year. Not a summary, not just the number — the full report, with your accounts, payment history and personal details.

That is the thing worth getting. The score on its own tells you nothing about *why*.

To get it, go to the bureau's own website and look for the free annual report option. You will be asked for your name, PAN, date of birth and mobile number, then taken through a short identity check. The report arrives on screen or by email, usually within a few minutes.

You can buy further copies at any time, but you do not need a paid subscription to find out what is wrong with your report.

## Apps that show you a "free score"

Plenty of banking apps and loan marketplaces will show you a credit score at no charge. There is nothing wrong with using them to keep half an eye on the number.

But understand what you are looking at. Most show a score and a broad summary — a few categories marked green or red. That is not the full report. It will not show you the month-by-month payment grid, the exact amounts and dates on each account, or the personal information section where the worst errors hide.

If your score is fine and you are just keeping watch, an app is enough. If your score is low and you want to know why, get the full report.

## Checking your own score does not harm it

This is probably the most common worry we hear, and the answer is simple: no, it does not.

When you look at your own credit report, that is recorded as a **soft enquiry**. Soft enquiries have no effect on your score, no matter how often you make them. You could check every week for a year and the number would be exactly what it would have been anyway.

What does count against you is a **hard enquiry** — the pull a lender makes when you actually apply for a loan or a credit card. Several hard enquiries in a short period make you look like you are hunting for credit, and that does weigh on the score.

So the sensible habit is the opposite of what most people do. Check your own report freely, especially before an important application. Apply to lenders sparingly.

## Do it safely

Your credit report is one of the most sensitive documents you own. A few habits protect you:

**Go to the bureau's website by typing the address yourself.** Do not use a link someone sent you on WhatsApp or SMS, and be careful with search advertisements. Fake credit-report sites exist for exactly this reason.

**Nobody legitimate needs your banking passwords.** Not the bureau, not your bank, not any service offering to help. If a website or a person asks for your net banking login, your card PIN, your UPI PIN or an OTP in order to "get your report", stop. The only OTP you should ever enter is the one the bureau sends to your own phone, on the bureau's own site, while you are downloading your own report.

**Do not let anyone else log in as you.** If someone offers to fetch your report using your credentials, refuse. Download it yourself and share the finished file if you need to.

## What to actually look at

Once you have the report open, resist the urge to look only at the number. Work through it in this order:

**The personal information section.** Almost nobody reads this, and it is where the worst problems start. Check your name spelling, PAN, date of birth, addresses and phone numbers. One wrong field is how a stranger's loan ends up attached to your file.

**The account summary.** How many accounts does it say you have — total, active, closed? Does that match what you remember borrowing? A number that does not match is the fastest way to spot a loan you never took, or one you closed years ago that is still counted as running.

**Each account in turn.** Lender name, account type, sanctioned amount or credit limit, and the opening and closing dates. Do the amounts match your papers? Is a closed loan still showing a balance? Is the same loan listed twice?

**The payment history grid.** The row of codes showing how many days late each month's payment was. Cross-check any late marks against your bank statement. People find marks here for months they know they paid on time.

**The enquiries section.** Every time a lender pulled your report. Applications you do not recognise are worth questioning.

## If you find something wrong

An error in a credit report is not rare, and it does not fix itself. The bureau will not notice it, and it will not drop off because time has passed.

What you can do is raise a dispute. Every bureau has an official dispute process, it is open to you directly, and it costs nothing to use. The bureau takes the matter up with the lender that reported the entry, and is required to investigate within 30 days of receiving your dispute.

Two honest warnings. First, a dispute needs a factual error — something that does not match what actually happened, and that a document can prove. Accurate information cannot be removed just because it is unflattering, whatever anyone tells you. Second, nobody can promise you what a correction will do to your score. That is calculated by the bureau from your whole credit history.
